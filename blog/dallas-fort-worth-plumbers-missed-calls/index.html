Dallas–Fort Worth Plumbers: Stop Losing Calls

Dallas–Fort Worth is one of the fastest-growing home-services markets in the United States — and one of the easiest places to lose a job to the next listing on Google. New subdivisions keep pushing out through Frisco, McKinney and Prosper, and every one of them needs a plumber. The problem is not finding work. It is answering the phone while you are already on a job.

Last updated 7 November 2026

Why DFW is different: growth, sprawl, and a plumber on every corner

Texas has 8,966 plumbing and HVAC establishments, according to the Census Bureau's County Business Patterns for 2023 — and 26,039 solo, nonemployer plumbing and HVAC businesses, the deepest pool of one-person shops in the country. That is the DFW market in two numbers: enormous demand, and enormous competition. A homeowner with a leak has a dozen plumbers within a short drive, and they will call them in order until one answers.

Growth makes it worse, not better. New subdivisions mean new kitchens, new bathrooms and new water heaters — and new customers with no plumber of their own yet. The first shop to answer wins a customer who may call back for twenty years. The shop that lets it ring gets nothing, not even a voicemail.

The DFW call pattern: summer AC, freeze-event plumbing spikes, storm work

DFW does not have a quiet season. Summer brings air-conditioning failures, water heaters working harder and slab leaks showing up in the heat. Then a freeze event hits, pipes burst across the metroplex in a single night, and every phone in town rings at once. Storm work fills the gaps.

The pattern matters because the spikes are when you miss the most calls. On a normal Tuesday you might answer nine out of ten. On the morning after a freeze, when three jobs are already booked and the phone will not stop, you answer far fewer — and those are the highest-value calls of the year, from homeowners who need someone today, not next week.

What Dallas homeowners do when you don't answer (they call the next listing)

Put yourself in the homeowner's shoes. Water is coming through the ceiling. They search, they tap the first number, it rings out. They do not leave a voicemail and wait — they tap the next number. Research on how consumers behave after an unanswered call (CallRail, 2025, 1,000 US consumers) found that 78% have abandoned a business after an unanswered call and 82% would call a competitor. Only 42% leave a voicemail at all.

Texas rules: SB140 quiet hours and what they mean for your texts

Texas has its own texting rules, and they are stricter than the federal ones in one way that matters. Under Texas SB140, in force from 1 September 2025, business texts may only go out between 9am and 9pm Monday to Saturday, and between noon and 9pm on Sunday. The federal quiet hours are 8am to 9pm in the recipient's local time. So a text that is legal under federal law can still break Texas law if it goes out at 8:30 on a Sunday morning.

The practical answer is to queue after-hours texts to the next morning's window: a missed call at 11pm gets a text at 9am, not at midnight — and the text should say so. This is general information, not legal advice: the rules are new, and a Texas telecom lawyer should review your setup before you rely on it.

The suburbs matter: Plano, Frisco, McKinney, Arlington, Irving

Do not think of DFW as one market. The growth — and the calls — are in the suburbs. Plano and Frisco have the new-build volume; McKinney and Prosper have the newest subdivisions; Arlington and Irving have the older housing stock that needs repipes, water heaters and drain work. Each suburb is a different job mix, and each one has its own set of competing plumbers.

The 1-week test: count your missed calls, then decide
