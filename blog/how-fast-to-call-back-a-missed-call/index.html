How Fast Should You Call Back a Missed Call?

A missed call is a race, and the clock starts the second the phone stops ringing. The first business to respond usually wins the job — so how fast is fast enough, and what should a two-person shop actually promise?

Last updated 7 November 2026

A missed call is a race, and the clock starts the second the phone stops ringing. The first business to respond usually wins the job — so how fast is fast enough, and what should a two-person shop actually promise?

The 5-minute rule: what the research actually measured (and its limits)

You have probably heard the “5-minute rule”: contact a lead within five minutes and you are far more likely to win it. The number behind it comes from an MIT/InsideSales study from 2007, which looked at more than 15,000 web leads. It found that contacting a lead within 5 minutes, rather than 30 minutes, made it 21 times more likely to qualify. A Harvard Business Review audit from 2011, covering 2,241 firms, found a similar pattern: firms that replied within an hour were about 7 times more likely to qualify a lead than firms that replied later, and the average response time across the firms studied was 42 hours.

Those numbers are striking, but read the label before you quote them. Both studies measured web leads in general, not home-service phone calls, and the figures are odds ratios, not close-rate multipliers. They do not mean you will close 21 times as many plumbing jobs by calling back in five minutes. What they do show, consistently, is direction: speed matters, and the gap between fast and slow is large. A 2026 answering-service survey cited in search results says 83% of US consumers choose the business that responds first — treat that as a vendor survey, not independent research, but it points the same way.

Why phone leads decay faster than web leads

A web lead fills in a form and waits. A phone caller is already holding a phone, standing next to a leak or a cold house, with your competitor one tap away on Google. If your voicemail answers instead of you, the caller does not sit and wait for a callback — they dial the next name on the list. That is why a missed phone call decays faster than a missed form fill: the caller’s next step takes seconds, not hours.

A callback rule a 2-person shop can actually keep (15 minutes in hours)

Forget five minutes as a promise. A two-person shop with both techs under sinks cannot guarantee a five-minute callback, and a promise you break is worse than a slower promise you keep. Set the rule you can actually keep: every missed call gets a reply within 15 minutes during working hours. Fifteen minutes is fast enough to catch most callers before they have booked elsewhere, and honest enough that your team can hit it on a busy day.

The text-first trick: when you can’t talk, text in 60 seconds

Sometimes you genuinely cannot talk. You are under a sink, in a panel, or on a roof. But you can almost always send a text, and a text in 60 seconds beats a phone call in two hours. A short, honest message — your business name, an apology for missing the call, and a booking link — tells the caller three things at once: you are a real business, you saw their call, and there is a next step that does not involve waiting.

This is exactly what RingBack’s missed-call text-back does automatically: the moment a call goes unanswered, the caller gets a text with a booking link, in about a minute. You do not have to stop the job, wash your hands, and find the phone. The text holds the caller while you finish what you are doing, and when you do call back, you are calling someone who already knows who you are and has a slot in mind.

What to say when you’re 2 hours late (honest, short, with a time)

Sooner or later you will be late — two hours late, or calling back the next morning. Do not pretend otherwise, and do not open with a long apology. Say what happened in one line, say when you can help in the next, and then stop talking. For example: “Sorry I missed you yesterday — I was on an emergency call-out. I can be with you Thursday between 9 and 11, or I can book you in now. Which suits you?”

The morning list: overnight callbacks before 8am, in order

Overnight missed calls need a routine, not good intentions. Before 8am, work the list in this order: emergencies first (no heat, no water, no power, active leak), then anyone who texted back, then everyone else in the order they called. Emergencies first is obvious, but the second group matters too — a caller who replied to your text has already raised their hand, and they are the closest thing to a booked job on the list.

How to measure it: time-to-first-reply, tracked weekly

You cannot improve a number you do not measure. Pick one number: time-to-first-reply, the minutes between a missed call and your first response, whether that is a call or a text. Once a week, take ten missed calls from the log, work out the time for each, and write down the median. The median matters more than the average, because one call returned three days later will wreck an average and hide a week of fast replies.

Frequently asked questions

How fast should I call back a missed call?

Within 15 minutes during working hours is a rule most small shops can actually keep. A text within 60 seconds is even better when you cannot talk — it holds the caller until you can call.

Is the 5-minute rule real?

The 5-minute figure comes from a 2007 MIT/InsideSales study of web leads in general, not home-service phone calls. It shows speed matters, but it is not a phone-call close rate — treat it as direction, not a guarantee.

What if I miss a call overnight?

Work the overnight list before 8am: emergencies first, then anyone who replied to your text, then everyone else in the order they called. One text with a booking link is enough if they do not answer.

Does a text count as a callback?

For the caller, yes — a fast text with a booking link is often better than a slow phone call. It proves you saw their call and gives them a next step immediately.
